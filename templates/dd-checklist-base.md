# Due Diligence Checklist — Base Template

> **Template.** This is the comprehensive ~50-point due diligence checklist base. The `modes/dd.md` instructions tailor this to the specific business archetype and risks flagged in the A-F evaluation report, then write the result to `reports/{NNN}-dd-checklist.md`. Each item lists **What to request**, **Why it matters**, and **What to look for**.

---

## How to Use

1. Read the A-F evaluation report for the business (`reports/{NNN}-{slug}-{date}.md`), especially the YAML footer (`key_risks`, `archetype`, `risk_tier`).
2. For each section below, include items relevant to the business. Add archetype-specific items (e.g., equipment age for asset-heavy businesses, customer concentration for route/service businesses).
3. Mark items as `[ ]` (not yet requested), `[~]` (requested, pending), or `[x]` (received and reviewed) in the final output.
4. For any item flagged as a red flag in the evaluation report, prioritize and annotate with the specific concern.

## Section A — Financial (16 items)

### A1. Three years of federal tax returns (business)
- **What to request:** Business federal tax returns (Forms 1120-S, 1120, or Schedule C) for the last three complete tax years.
- **Why it matters:** Tax returns are the most reliable independent confirmation of revenue and profit claims. Sellers cannot easily restate filed returns.
- **What to look for:** Revenue and SDE consistency year over year. Any large one-time deductions, shareholder loans, or related-party transactions. Discrepancies between tax returns and the broker's P&L.

### A2. Three years of state tax returns (business)
- **What to request:** State income/franchise/sales tax returns for the last three years.
- **Why it matters:** Confirms state-level compliance and sales tax collection for retail businesses.
- **What to look for:** Unpaid sales tax liabilities, state filing gaps, nexus issues.

### A3. Year-to-date P&L (current year)
- **What to request:** Profit & Loss statement through the most recent month-end for the current year.
- **Why it matters:** Confirms the business is on track relative to prior years. Stale listings often show deteriorating current-year performance.
- **What to look for:** Revenue trending below prior year, margin compression, unusual expense spikes.

### A4. Quarterly P&Ls — prior 24 months
- **What to request:** Quarterly P&Ls for the last 24 months (not just annual).
- **Why it matters:** Reveals seasonality and any single-quarter anomalies hidden in annual averages.
- **What to look for:** Quarter-over-quarter volatility, seasonality pattern, any "one-time" expenses that recur.

### A5. Add-back support documentation
- **What to request:** Itemized list of every add-back the seller used to compute SDE, with supporting receipts or memos.
- **Why it matters:** Add-backs are the #1 source of inflated SDE in small business listings. Personal expenses, one-time expenses, and owner salary are commonly overstated.
- **What to look for:** Owner salary above replacement cost. Personal auto, travel, meals, insurance written off as business. One-time expenses that are actually recurring (e.g., a roof repair every 5 years).

### A6. Twelve months of business bank statements
- **What to request:** Monthly bank statements for the primary operating account(s) for the last 12 months.
- **Why it matters:** Bank statements are harder to falsify than P&Ls. Revenue deposits should reconcile to claimed revenue.
- **What to look for:** Deposit pattern consistent with claimed revenue. Unexplained large transfers to personal accounts. Multiple merchant processor deposits (could indicate commingled revenue).

### A7. Balance sheet — most recent
- **What to request:** Current balance sheet (assets, liabilities, equity).
- **Why it matters:** Reveals hidden liabilities, working capital position, and asset quality.
- **What to look for:** Negative equity, large accounts receivable that may not be collectible, accrued liabilities, shareholder loans payable to owner (often need to be paid off at closing).

### A8. Accounts Receivable aging report
- **What to request:** AR aging report (current, 30/60/90+ days) as of the most recent month-end.
- **Why it matters:** AR collectibility directly affects working capital and the real purchase price.
- **What to look for:** Concentration of AR in 90+ day buckets. AR from related parties. AR significantly exceeding historical collection rate.

### A9. Accounts Payable aging report
- **What to request:** AP aging report as of the most recent month-end.
- **Why it matters:** Uncovers accrued obligations the buyer may inherit and working capital pressure.
- **What to look for:** AP significantly overdue (60+ days), which can indicate cash flow distress or vendor disputes.

### A10. Sales tax / payroll tax liability verification
- **What to request:** Confirmation from seller that all sales tax and payroll tax deposits have been remitted through the most recent quarter.
- **Why it matters:** Unpaid trust-fund taxes (sales tax, payroll withholding) can pierce the corporate veil and follow the buyer in many states.
- **What to look for:** Any open liability for sales tax, withholding, or unemployment insurance.

### A11. Credit card statements — 12 months
- **What to request:** Business credit card statements for the last 12 months.
- **Why it matters:** Many add-backs and personal expenses flow through credit cards. Confirms expenses claimed on the P&L.
- **What to look for:** Personal charges, recurring subscriptions for services the buyer may not retain, charges that don't reconcile to the P&L.

### A12. Cash flow reconciliation / cash basis P&L
- **What to request:** A cash-basis view of revenue and expenses, or a reconciliation between accrual P&L and cash movement.
- **Why it matters:** Accrual P&Ls can overstate earnings by deferring expenses. Cash reconciliation shows the real money.
- **What to look for:** Large gap between accrual net income and actual cash generated. Non-cash revenue entries.

### A13. Merchant processor statements — 12 months
- **What to request:** Monthly statements from each credit card processor / POS system.
- **Why it matters:** Independent third-party confirmation of revenue, especially for retail and food service businesses.
- **What to look for:** Total processed volume reconciles to claimed revenue. Chargeback rate (high rate indicates customer disputes or fraud).

### A14. Insurance loss runs — 3 years
- **What to request:** Loss run reports from the business's property, liability, and workers' comp insurers for the last 3 years.
- **Why it matters:** Uninsured claims history affects future premiums and reveals operational risks (e.g., frequent slip-and-fall claims at a retail location).
- **What to look for:** Claim frequency and severity trends. Workers' comp claims (indicates safety culture).

### A15. Owner's W-2s / K-1s — 3 years
- **What to request:** Owner's personal W-2 or K-1 from the business for the last 3 years.
- **Why it matters:** Confirms the actual compensation the owner takes, separate from claimed add-backs.
- **What to look for:** Compensation substantially below replacement cost, which means the buyer will need to pay more to replace the operator.

### A16. Forecasted next-12-month P&L (if available)
- **What to request:** Any internal projections or forecasts the seller has prepared.
- **Why it matters:** Reveals the seller's own view of the business trajectory and any known risks.
- **What to look for:** Conservative vs. aggressive assumptions. Any known headwinds (lost customer, expiring contract) the seller anticipates.

## Section B — Operational (14 items)

### B1. Equipment inventory with age and condition
- **What to request:** Complete equipment list with make, model, year acquired, condition, and current fair market value.
- **Why it matters:** For asset-heavy businesses (laundromat, car wash, auto repair), equipment age is the single largest valuation driver and a hidden capex liability.
- **What to look for:** Average equipment age approaching end-of-life. Proprietary or hard-to-source equipment. Equipment leased rather than owned.

### B2. Equipment maintenance logs
- **What to request:** Maintenance records, service contracts, and recent repair invoices for major equipment.
- **Why it matters:** Deferred maintenance is a hidden capex liability. Well-maintained equipment commands a premium.
- **What to look for:** Gaps in maintenance history. Recurring breakdowns on the same equipment. Service contracts recently cancelled (often done before sale to inflate SDE).

### B3. Employee roster with tenure and roles
- **What to request:** List of all employees, including role, hire date, full-time/part-time status, and current wage.
- **Why it matters:** Employee retention after acquisition is critical. Key employees who leave can devastate a service business.
- **What to look for:** High turnover. A single key employee holding critical operational knowledge. Employees paid below market (retention risk post-close).

### B4. Payroll records — 12 months
- **What to request:** Payroll registers for the last 12 months, including overtime, bonuses, and owner payroll.
- **Why it matters:** Confirms headcount claims and identifies shadow payroll (owner family on the payroll).
- **What to look for:** Family members on payroll. Owner's true compensation. Overtime patterns indicating understaffing.

### B5. Customer list with revenue concentration
- **What to request:** Customer list with annual revenue per customer for the last 12 months.
- **Why it matters:** Customer concentration is a primary transferability risk. If any single customer exceeds 15% of revenue, the business is at risk of a catastrophic loss.
- **What to look for:** Any customer >15% of revenue (flag as a hard blocker or negotiation point). Customer tenure. Geographic concentration.

### B6. Customer contracts and agreements
- **What to request:** Copies of all written customer contracts, service agreements, and recurring revenue arrangements.
- **Why it matters:** Contracted revenue is more valuable than walk-in revenue. Assignability of contracts affects post-close revenue stability.
- **What to look for:** Auto-renewal vs. cancellation terms. Personal-service clauses that may void on sale. Pricing terms relative to market.

### B7. Supplier / vendor contracts
- **What to request:** All supplier agreements, pricing schedules, and any exclusive arrangements.
- **Why it matters:** Supplier relationships can carry pricing advantages that may or may not transfer. Single-supplier dependencies are a transferability risk.
- **What to look for:** Single-source suppliers with no alternative. Pricing that depends on the seller's personal relationship. Supplier contracts that terminate on change of control.

### B8. Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)
- **What to request:** Any documented SOPs, training manuals, and operational workflows.
- **Why it matters:** Documented processes make the business transferable and reduce key-man risk. Lack of SOPs is a red flag for an owner-operator business.
- **What to look for:** Whether SOPs exist at all. Whether they are current. Whether key processes are owner-dependent (i.e., only the owner knows how to do them).

### B9. Lease agreement (premises) with amendments
- **What to request:** Complete lease agreement and all amendments, plus the most recent rent reconciliation.
- **Why it matters:** Lease is the single most common deal-killer in main street acquisitions. Short remaining term, no renewal options, or no assignability can kill a deal.
- **What to look for:** Remaining lease term (target ≥3 years). Renewal option terms. Rent as % of gross revenue (target <10% for most categories). Personal guaranty requirements for the buyer.

### B10. Landlord assignability confirmation
- **What to request:** Written confirmation from the landlord (or a clause in the lease) on assignability terms.
- **Why it matters:** Even an assignable lease may require landlord consent, which can introduce new rent, guaranty, or term conditions.
- **What to look for:** Landlord's stated requirements for assignment. Any required rent escalation on assignment. Personal guaranty demands.

### B11. Inventory count and valuation
- **What to request:** Most recent physical inventory count with valuation method (FIFO, LIFO, cost).
- **Why it matters:** Inventory is often included in the purchase price; stale or obsolete inventory inflates the apparent deal value.
- **What to look for:** Slow-moving or obsolete inventory. Inventory turnover rate vs. industry norms. Inventory value as % of asking price.

### B12. Vehicle titles and liens
- **What to request:** Titles for all vehicles used in the business, plus UCC search results for any liens.
- **Why it matters:** Vehicles with liens must be paid off or assumed at closing. Missing titles indicate ownership disputes.
- **What to look for:** Liens on titled equipment. Personal-use vehicles mixed into the business. Vehicles older than useful life.

### B13. Technology stack and software licenses
- **What to request:** List of all software, POS systems, accounting platforms, and SaaS subscriptions used.
- **Why it matters:** Software is often owner-licensed personally. Transferring licenses (and their data) post-close can be costly.
- **What to look for:** Software licensed to the owner personally rather than the business. Critical operational data stored in personal accounts.

### B14. Marketing and customer acquisition channels
- **What to request:** Summary of marketing channels, spend, and customer acquisition cost by channel.
- **Why it matters:** The buyer must be able to maintain the customer acquisition engine post-close. Owner-dependent marketing (personal relationships, personal reputation) is a transferability risk.
- **What to look for:** Customer acquisition cost trends. Reliance on a single channel. Reviews and online reputation.

## Section C — Legal (12 items)

### C1. Entity formation documents
- **What to request:** Articles of incorporation/organization, operating agreement, bylaws, and any amendments.
- **Why it matters:** Confirms the seller has clear authority to sell and that the entity is in good standing.
- **What to look for:** Ownership structure, any minority owners who must consent, and authority to sell provisions.

### C2. Business licenses and permits
- **What to request:** All current federal, state, county, and municipal licenses and permits required to operate.
- **Why it matters:** Many licenses are non-transferable and must be re-applied for by the buyer. Operating without a valid license post-close is illegal.
- **What to look for:** Expiration dates. Personal licenses (e.g., contractor, cosmetology, food handler) tied to the owner. Renewal requirements.

### C3. Lease agreement — assignability clause
- **What to request:** Highlighted assignability clause from the lease (overlaps with B9 — focus on legal mechanics here).
- **Why it matters:** Even with a transferable lease, the legal mechanics of assignment affect closing cost and timing.
- **What to look for:** Consent requirements, conditions precedent, and any landlord right to terminate on assignment.

### C4. Pending or threatened litigation
- **What to request:** List of any pending, threatened, or recently settled litigation, claims, or regulatory actions.
- **Why it matters:** Litigation may follow the business post-close depending on structure. Even asset deals can inherit product liability and employment claims.
- **What to look for:** Employment claims (often follow the business), customer disputes, regulatory enforcement actions.

### C5. Insurance policies in force
- **What to request:** Declarations pages for all current insurance policies (general liability, property, workers' comp, auto, EPLI, cyber).
- **Why it matters:** Confirms adequate coverage and informs the buyer's post-close insurance procurement.
- **What to look for:** Coverage limits, deductibles, and any policy cancellations or non-renewals pending.

### C6. UCC lien search
- **What to request:** UCC-1 search results for the business entity and the owner personally, in the state of incorporation and operation.
- **Why it matters:** Unreleased UCC filings on business assets must be cleared at closing or the buyer inherits secured claims on the purchased assets.
- **What to look for:** Any active UCC-1 filings. Equipment financing liens. SBA liens that may need payoff letters.

### C7. Restrictive covenants / non-competes from employees
- **What to request:** Copies of any non-compete, non-solicit, or confidentiality agreements signed by employees or the seller.
- **Why it matters:** Without enforceable non-competes, the seller could open a competing business nearby and take customers post-close.
- **What to look for:** Whether the seller has signed a non-compete (should be a condition of closing). Reasonableness of scope and duration.

### C8. Seller non-compete agreement (draft)
- **What to request:** A draft seller non-compete, typically 2–5 years, scoped to the business's trade area.
- **Why it matters:** A seller non-compete is a standard closing document and a key protection of the purchase price. Without it, the seller can re-enter the market.
- **What to look for:** Geographic scope, duration, and enforceability under state law. (This is a closing deliverable, not a diligence item per se — flag if the seller resists.)

### C9. Intellectual property assignments
- **What to request:** Trademark registrations, domain registrations, and any IP assignment documents.
- **Why it matters:** IP must be affirmatively assigned in the Definitive Agreement; ownership must be clear pre-close.
- **What to look for:** Whether trademarks are registered in the owner's name personally (must be assigned). Domain registration in the owner's name.

### C10. Workers' comp claims history
- **What to request:** Workers' comp experience modifier (X-Mod) and claims history for the last 3 years.
- **Why it matters:** High X-Mod indicates unsafe operations and inflates future premiums. Claims history is a leading indicator of operational risk.
- **What to look for:** X-Mod above 1.0 (industry average). Claim frequency. Any open claims that may follow the business.

### C11. Environmental compliance (if applicable)
- **What to request:** Phase I ESA, environmental permits, and any prior environmental assessments (especially for auto repair, dry cleaning, gas stations, car washes).
- **Why it matters:** Environmental contamination can create cleanup liability that follows the property or the business. Asset purchases offer some protection but not total.
- **What to look for:** Historical use of the property, any open environmental permits, and any past contamination reports.

### C12. Privacy, data, and customer data handling
- **What to request:** Summary of how customer data is collected, stored, and used. Any breach notifications.
- **Why it matters:** The buyer inherits data privacy obligations. A breach history may create post-close liability.
- **What to look for:** Whether PII/PCI is stored locally. Any breach notifications sent. GDPR/CCPA applicability.

## Section D — Market & Strategic (8 items)

### D1. Competitor map
- **What to request:** Seller's understanding of direct and indirect competitors within the trade area, with relative market share.
- **Why it matters:** Competitive intensity affects sustainable margins and growth. A saturated market limits pricing power.
- **What to look for:** Number of direct competitors within 5 miles. New entrants or recent closures. Price-led vs. service-led competitors.

### D2. Market trend data
- **What to request:** Any third-party market research, IBISWorld reports, or industry data the seller has.
- **Why it matters:** A business in a declining market is a value trap regardless of current cash flow.
- **What to look for:** Industry growth rate. Demographic tailwinds or headwinds in the local market. Disruption risk (e.g., e-commerce for retail).

### D3. Customer reviews and reputation
- **What to request:** Google, Yelp, BBB, and any industry-specific review profiles for the business.
- **Why it matters:** Online reputation is a leading indicator of customer retention and pricing power.
- **What to look for:** Average rating, review volume, response patterns. Any recurring complaint themes (e.g., pricing, service quality, owner-related).

### D4. Customer retention rate
- **What to request:** Customer retention rate or repeat-customer percentage, with supporting data.
- **Why it matters:** Retention confirms whether revenue is durable or dependent on continuous acquisition.
- **What to look for:** Retention trends (improving or declining). Concentration of revenue in long-tenured customers.

### D5. Pricing power analysis
- **What to request:** Price increase history and the seller's view on pricing elasticity.
- **Why it matters:** Pricing power is a value-creation lever the buyer can pull post-close. A business that hasn't raised prices in years has hidden margin.
- **What to look for:** Frequency and magnitude of price increases. Pricing relative to competitors. Any customer contracts that lock pricing.

### D6. Referral and word-of-mouth pipeline
- **What to request:** Percentage of new customers from referrals vs. paid channels.
- **Why it matters:** A referral-driven business has lower customer acquisition cost and is more transferable.
- **What to look for:** Referral share trends. Whether referrals are tied to the owner personally (a transferability risk).

### D7. Local economic and demographic data
- **What to request:** Recent Census/ACS data, employment trends, and any local economic development reports.
- **Why it matters:** Local economic trajectory affects the business for years post-close. A declining local market is a structural headwind.
- **What to look for:** Population growth, income growth, employment growth in the trade area. Major employer openings or closures.

### D8. Strategic / add-on acquisition opportunities
- **What to request:** Seller's view of any bolt-on acquisition targets or expansion opportunities in the market.
- **Why it matters:** Identifies post-close value-creation levers and validates that the seller has thought about growth (a good sign) or is exiting because growth is exhausted (a caution sign).
- **What to look for:** Specific named opportunities. Whether the seller's reluctance to pursue them indicates capital or skill constraints (which the buyer can solve) or market exhaustion (which the buyer cannot).

## Section E — Closing & Transition (5 items)

### E1. Transition support agreement
- **What to request:** Seller's proposed transition support period (typically 30–90 days post-close).
- **Why it matters:** A smooth handoff protects customer and employee relationships. Free or paid transition support affects the effective purchase price.
- **What to look for:** Length of support, compensation (free vs. paid), scope (training, customer introductions, vendor introductions).

### E2. Working capital target confirmation
- **What to request:** Agreement on the working capital peg and methodology for the closing-day calculation.
- **Why it matters:** The working capital adjustment can move the effective purchase price by 5–15%. Ambiguity here creates closing-day disputes.
- **What to look for:** Clear definition of included line items. 12-month average vs. closing-day snapshot. Dispute resolution mechanism.

### E3. Allocation of purchase price
- **What to request:** Preliminary purchase price allocation across asset classes (FF&E, inventory, non-compete, goodwill).
- **Why it matters:** Allocation affects the buyer's tax basis and depreciation schedule and the seller's capital gains treatment.
- **What to look for:** Reasonable allocation that both parties can accept on Form 8594.

### E4. Key employee retention agreements
- **What to request:** Retention bonus or stay-bonus offers for any key employees identified during diligence.
- **Why it matters:** Losing a key employee during the transition can damage the business. Retention agreements are a closing cost.
- **What to look for:** Whether retention bonuses are paid by seller pre-close or buyer post-close. Vesting/clawback terms.

### E5. Bulk sale / transfer tax clearance
- **What to request:** State bulk-sale notice filings and any required transfer tax clearances.
- **Why it matters:** Skipping bulk-sale notice can leave the buyer liable for the seller's unpaid sales or payroll taxes in some states.
- **What to look for:** State-specific requirements. Whether escrow is handling this or the buyer must file directly.
